**Recommendation: prototype truthful status with a persistent return path.** Show the actual queued, generating, ready, or failed state; explain that generation continues after leaving; let consultants find the same job in Drafts. Compare a compact status card with a visible sequence of real stages. Defer percentage completion and a published duration range until the service can substantiate them.

**Research and decision date: 5 September 2026 (UTC).** This is a provisional design recommendation. PitchDraft’s context is hypothetical: there are no customer observations, and “20 seconds to 4 minutes” is an engineering estimate, not a measured distribution, service guarantee, or verified upper bound. Whether consultants want to switch tasks remains a hypothesis.

**What the options permit**

| Direction | Judgment and condition |
|---|---|
| Percentage bar | **Defer.** No reliable denominator exists in the brief. Elapsed time divided by four minutes would invent completion. Reconsider when actual work progress can be measured; completed-work percentage still need not equal remaining-time percentage. Carbon supports this distinction in choosing a determinate indicator. |
| Estimated time range | **Potentially useful, presently unsupported.** A range could help someone choose another task, but “usually 20 seconds–4 minutes” would turn an estimate into an observed claim. None of the inspected studies establishes an appropriate range for this workflow. Measure queue-plus-generation time and overruns before publishing one; do not silently count down an uncalibrated estimate. Microsoft’s accuracy condition applies. |
| Named stages and return path | **Proceed to prototype.** Map labels to actual events: Waiting to start → Generating draft → Ready; Failed is a separate outcome. Avoid invented “researching/checking/polishing” stages or equally filled segments implying equal duration. The transparency study offers adjacent support; background-task guidance and the supplied persistence constraint support the return path. |
| Simpler alternative | **Use as the comparison baseline:** current-state text, a modest indeterminate indicator, and a persistent Drafts entry. Elapsed time could be tested later if people need duration context; it is not evidence that a job remains healthy. |

Proposed copy after the job is accepted: “Your draft will keep generating if you leave this page. Find it in Drafts.” Provide a named draft link and “Back to Drafts.” Keep ready/failed status available on return; show a clear recovery action for failure, conditional on retry support. Announce status changes accessibly without moving focus. These are proposed interactions, not observed needs or validated results.

**Smallest useful comparison in three design days**

1. **Day 1:** Build one clickable flow with two status treatments: A, compact current-state card; B, the same card plus the real stage sequence. Keep leaving, Drafts navigation, completion, and failure identical. This isolates whether the stage sequence adds useful understanding.
2. **Day 2:** Run four formative sessions with consultants who create proposals, if recruitment is available; otherwise label colleague sessions as a pilot. Counterbalance A/B order. Use matched four-minute simulated jobs and a realistic secondary task, allowing participants to choose whether to leave. Include short walkthroughs of a 20-second completion, returning after completion, failure, and a job still generating beyond four minutes. These are test scenarios, not latency samples.
3. **Day 3:** Revise from observed misunderstandings. Record whether each person understands that leaving preserves the job, finds the same draft unaided, distinguishes waiting from failure, and interprets stages as completion percentages. Ask what informed their choice to stay or leave; preference alone is insufficient.

**Proposed decision rule:** retain the stage sequence only if it adds useful comprehension without implying timing or blocking departure; otherwise choose the simpler card. Any belief that leaving cancels work, or inability to recover the draft, triggers revision. Four sessions identify usability issues, not population preference or conversion lift. Problem prevalence is unknown; uncertainty reduction is plausible; this intervention’s effectiveness is untested. Collect end-to-end latency and job-to-draft reopening data before choosing timing promises or treating page departure as abandonment.
